Introduction

The history of the Internet began with research into packet-switched computer networks during the 1960s. Early projects such as ARPANET demonstrated that computers located far apart could communicate and share resources through a network. During the 1970s and 1980s, the development and adoption of TCP/IP allowed different networks to interconnect, creating a network of networks. The expansion of NSFNET and other interconnected networks helped transform the Internet from a research system into a global communications infrastructure used by universities, organizations, businesses, and the public.

Key Milestones (1969 – 2012)

1969: First ARPANET Connection

On October 29, 1969, computers at UCLA and Stanford connected for the first time, establishing the foundational network of the Internet. This proved that long-distance packet networking was physically possible.

Source: Computer History Museum

1971: Network Email

Programmer Ray Tomlinson implemented the first system to send mail between users on different ARPANET hosts, introducing the "@" symbol. It shifted the network's purpose toward direct human-to-human communication.

Source: Internet Society

1973: Global Expansion

The UK and Norway connected to ARPANET, expanding the network into an international endeavor and proving that connections could cross oceans.

Source: IETF Standards

1974: TCP Networking Proposal

Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn published their design for the Transmission Control Protocol (TCP), describing how different packet-switched networks could communicate with one another. This work became a foundation for the TCP/IP protocol suite used by the Internet.

Source:Internet Society

1983: Transition to TCP/IP

On January 1, 1983, ARPANET transitioned from its previous Network Control Protocol (NCP) to the TCP/IP protocol suite. This allowed different networks to communicate using a common set of protocols and was a major step in the development of the modern Internet.

Source:Internet Society

1984: Domain Name System (DNS)

Paul Mockapetris introduced DNS, a naming system allowing users to type human-readable addresses instead of complex numeric IP addresses. This made network navigation practical for everyday users.

Source: Internet Society

1986: NSFNET Launch

NSFNET was launched to connect research and university networks across the United States. It became an important backbone of the growing Internet and helped expand network access beyond ARPANET.

Source: National Science Foundation (NSF)

1989: ARPANET Decommissioned

ARPANET was formally decommissioned after two decades of operation. By this time, TCP/IP-based networks had expanded significantly and ARPANET's functions had been absorbed into the growing Internet.

Source:Internet Society

1995: NSFNET Backbone Retired

The NSFNET backbone was retired as commercial Internet providers took over much of the network infrastructure. This marked an important transition toward the commercial and publicly accessible Internet.

Source: National Science Foundation (NSF)

2012: World IPv6 Launch

Internet providers, websites, and technology companies permanently enabled IPv6 as part of World IPv6 Launch. IPv6 provides a much larger address space to support the continuing growth of Internet-connected devices.

Source:Internet Society

Three Key Technologies

  • TCP/IP (Transmission Control Protocol / Internet Protocol): The foundational communication language of the Internet. It dictates how data is broken into packets, addressed, transmitted, routed, and reassembled at the destination, allowing vastly different computer networks to communicate seamlessly.
  • DNS (Domain Name System): Essentially the phonebook of the Internet. It translates memorable, human-readable domain names into the numerical IP addresses required by computers to locate services and devices worldwide.
  • Packet Switching: A networking technique in which data is divided into smaller packets that can travel independently through a network and be reassembled at the destination. Packet switching made efficient and flexible communication across interconnected computer networks possible.
